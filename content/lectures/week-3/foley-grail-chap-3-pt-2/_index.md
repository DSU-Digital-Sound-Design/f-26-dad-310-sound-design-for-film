## Style changes the choice

- Name one audible action from each scene
- Compare its prominence and character
- Explain what that choice does for the scene

{{% note %}}
Pair discussion, then two reports. Ask for evidence before accepting labels such as realistic or exaggerated. A useful response connects an action, an audible quality, and a dramatic purpose. If students disagree, replay the brief moment rather than settle the question by taste.

Transition: style affects both the performance and how that performance combines with other sounds.
{{%/ note %}}

## Foley alone or layered

- **Production audio:** recorded during the shoot
- **Foley:** actions performed to match the picture
- **Edited effects:** selected or designed sounds placed in the scene

A finished event may combine these sources.

{{% note %}}
The chapter calls Foley combined with other effects “married” Foley. Put the ordinary meaning first: layers working together. These are workflow distinctions, not a reliable listening test for a final mix.

Foley can replace missing action sounds or blend with usable production audio. It does not have to reproduce every sound captured on set. Supporting reality describes an intended effect on the audience, not a recording source.

Practitioner reference: https://www.shure.com/en-US/insights/everything-you-need-to-know-about-foley-recording

Keep this conceptual. Recording, editing, and mix workflow belong to later lessons. Assignment 1 is live performance; Assignment 2 adds recording and layering.
{{%/ note %}}

## What would each layer contribute?

For a heavy landing, propose:

- A performed sound for contact and movement
- A second sound for added weight

What could become excessive?

{{% note %}}
This is a hypothetical design exercise, not a breakdown of the preceding film. Students might propose a short scrape or rattle with a separate low thump. Ask what each contributes and whether the combination still fits the action.

If props are available, tap or move an object, then add a second performed layer. Keep the comparison at a comfortable level. Students should not perform body falls.
{{%/ note %}}

## A performed solution

The chapter describes footsteps with different pitches.

How would you perform a similar idea using objects available to you?

{{% note %}}
Ament's chapter recounts Foley artist John Roesch and mixer Tim Sadler developing musical footsteps. David Stone recalled tubing as the possible source and varying pitches for individual steps. Preserve the uncertainty: the recollection is not a verified inventory of materials.

The account says the team considered combining Foley with another designed effect, but recalled relying on the Foley itself. Do not present that recollection as an isolated-track demonstration.

Ask students to revise one earlier proposal based on the example. Allow a brief test if objects are available; otherwise save the physical trial for the robot task.
{{%/ note %}}

## Two objects, one action

- Perform the same robot event with two objects
- Keep the timing and gesture comparable
- Choose one and explain what fits better

{{% note %}}
Allow about 6 minutes including brief peer feedback. Use available classroom objects that can be handled safely and without damage. Test against the animation, not just in isolation. No recording is required.

A response should identify the action, the difference heard, and the reason for the choice: material, weight, mechanism, or personality. If an intended prop is unavailable, test two available substitutes and note what must be retested at rehearsal. Keep sounds at comfortable listening levels.
{{%/ note %}}

---

## Coverage and emphasis

This exercise requires every sound-making event.

Give important actions more emphasis while keeping the full event list covered.

{{% note %}}
Spend about 2 minutes resolving the apparent conflict with the dance examples. Professional soundtracks may selectively omit or de-emphasize action sounds. Assignment 1 deliberately requires coverage to practice event recognition, timing, and coordination. Coverage does not mean equal loudness.

Connect to the rubric: source choice 12 points; event coverage 10; synchronization 10; sense of life 4; team coordination 4. Do not expand coverage into every visible action regardless of whether it would make sound.
{{%/ note %}}

## Your rehearsal plan

- Assign each event and prop to a performer
- Identify the hardest handoff
- Agree when to rehearse with the actual objects

{{% note %}}
Use the remaining 3 minutes of the assignment block. Have each team write a practical plan they can take away. For a missed event or collision, decide whether to change the division of work or the object, then try again.

Rehearsal should use the same animation and actual props. A candidate object is only a proposal until the team can perform it in sync. Remind students: live performances are Monday, September 21.
