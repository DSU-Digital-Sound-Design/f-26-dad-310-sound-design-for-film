# Space

Acoustics, silence, and the frequency spectrum

Tim Harrison, *Sound Design for Film*, chapter 11

---

## Today’s goals

- Describe the audible clues that suggest a space or listening perspective.

- Propose a sound change and explain how it would affect a scene.

## Acoustic space

- Echo is an audible, separate repeat.
- Reverberation is a dense collection of reflections that decays over time.
- The balance of direct sound and reflections helps suggest distance and enclosure.

{{% note %}}

Room size, surfaces, and source and microphone positions all matter. A closer microphone generally captures more direct sound relative to the room. Close does not automatically mean dry, and outdoor sound can include reflections. Neither echo nor reverb has a fixed emotional meaning.

{{%/ note %}}

## Spectral perspective

- Frequency balance changes a sound’s brightness, weight, and clarity.
- Filtering can suggest a barrier, playback device, or subjective listening perspective.
- Masking happens when one sound makes another harder to hear.

{{% note %}}

Connect this to EQ experience without assuming everyone took DAD 222. Filtering and masking are different mechanisms. A muffled sound alone does not tell us its cause; the image and surrounding sounds supply context. Low frequencies do not automatically mean danger.

{{%/ note %}}

## Negative space

A sparse soundtrack leaves room for particular sounds to stand out.

- What has been removed or reduced?
- What remains audible?
- How does the contrast change our attention or sense of time?

{{% note %}}

Silence can be relative: reduced dialogue, effects, ambience, or music rather than a completely empty track. Meaning comes from context and contrast. Sparse sound can support calm, tension, intimacy, or other responses.

{{%/ note %}}

## Oppenheimer

What remains audible while we wait for the blast, and how does the blast change the scene?

<iframe width="560" height="315" src="https://www.youtube.com/embed/tK0IDmSYYGk" title="Oppenheimer" frameborder="0" allow="accelerometer; autoplay; clipboard-write; encrypted-media; gyroscope; picture-in-picture" allowfullscreen></iframe>

{{% note %}}

Play a short excerpt spanning the flash, the wait, and the arrival of the blast. First identify what is reduced and what remains audible. Then discuss how the contrast affects anticipation and impact. The quiet interval need not be completely silent. Light reaches an observer before sound, which motivates the delayed blast. The filmmakers still choose the timing, perspective, and sounds heard during the wait. Ask students to distinguish that physical basis from the creative construction of the sequence. Do not infer a true viewing distance from the timing of an edited film. Sound-team reference: https://www.asoundeffect.com/oppenheimer-film-sound/

{{%/ note %}}

## Silence before an impact

Reducing sound before an impact can make the arrival feel more forceful.

Reverb pre-delay is different. It is the interval between the direct sound and the onset of reverberation.

{{% note %}}

Connect this distinction to the Trinity excerpt. The wait before the blast is a dramatic pause, not a reverb parameter. No plugin demonstration is needed.

{{%/ note %}}

## Sicario

How do space, frequency balance, and quiet-to-loud contrast work together at the border?

<iframe width="560" height="315" src="https://www.youtube.com/embed/Uz0IOW8fV64?si=QeQMUCgtm3iB1gzU" title="Sicario" frameborder="0" allow="accelerometer; autoplay; clipboard-write; encrypted-media; gyroscope; picture-in-picture; web-share" referrerpolicy="strict-origin-when-cross-origin" allowfullscreen></iframe>

{{% note %}}

Use a short excerpt spanning the wait inside the vehicle and the confrontation. Allow five minutes for viewing and discussion. Ask students to identify one audible spatial clue, one change in frequency balance, and one contrast in sound density or level. Then ask how those choices direct attention. Compare their evidence with Citizen Kane, Come and See, and Oppenheimer. Treat this as a closing application of the lesson rather than another new technique.

{{%/ note %}}

## Redesign a scene

Choose one film excerpt from today. Work individually.

1. Name an audible detail in the original.
2. Propose one change to its room sound, frequency balance, or use of silence.
3. Explain how that change would affect the audience's experience.

Make a few notes, then share your idea. No separate submission.

{{% note %}}

Allow eight minutes. Students are proposing a change, not editing audio. Ask them to connect a specific sound choice to a predicted effect. For example, how would Citizen Kane read with much drier voices? Different interpretations are welcome when students support them with audible evidence.

{{%/ note %}}

---

## Discuss and check

Share a few redesigns with the class.

What would the audience hear differently, and why would that matter?

Before you leave, explain one difference between changing the room sound and changing the frequency balance.

{{% note %}}

Use the final five minutes. Ask students to describe the audible change before its intended effect. Accept that real spaces affect frequency balance as well as reflections. The distinction here is between design choices, not a claim that the processes are independent.

{{%/ note %}}
