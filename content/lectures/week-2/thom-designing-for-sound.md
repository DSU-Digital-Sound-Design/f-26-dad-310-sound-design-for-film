# Designing for Sound by Randy Thom

Read the essay before class: [Designing A Movie For Sound](http://filmsound.org/articles/designing_for_sound.htm) (1999). This page pulls out the arguments we discuss and pairs each one with a clip. All quotes are Thom's own words.

## What is sound design?

> The biggest myth about composing and sound designing is that they are about creating great sounds. Not true, or at least not true enough.

Thom's target is the idea that a sound designer's job starts after the film is shot. His alternative: design the film itself with sound in mind, so that sound can shape decisions in every other craft. He saves his answer to the title question for the last page, and so do we.

- Clip: *Inception* was written around a sound. "Non, Je Ne Regrette Rien" is the timing device inside the story, and Zimmer built the score out of the same track slowed down. [Watch](https://youtu.be/NNm8f3tIfAA?si=37L8TkestUPNzh-6)

Discussion: what would it mean for a sound to be "great" but useless to the film?

## Thom's three preconditions

The essay names three conditions a film must meet before sound can contribute much of anything:

1. Tell the story through the point of view of one or more characters.
2. Choose locations and design sets that encourage sound rather than rule it out.
3. Leave room in the dialogue. In his words: "If your intention is to pack your film with wall-to-wall clever dialog, maybe you should consider doing a play."

> Most of the great sound sequences in films are "pov" sequences.

- Clip: *The Diving Bell and the Butterfly* opens locked inside a character's perception, with vision and hearing both damaged. [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_6up-uz7Q9k)
- Clip: *Saving Private Ryan*'s Omaha Beach sequence hands the whole battle to one man's hearing. The muffling after the explosion is a point of view, not an effect. [Watch](https://youtu.be/hdBEyitJ7Qc?si=C9kYkYYGNie9a4_Z)
- Clip: *Black Swan* lets us hear what the protagonist hears as her grip slips, so real and imagined sound stop being separable. [Watch](https://youtu.be/mCmY7Q8wwts?si=A_EZC_RkeZOwLKM3)
- Clip: *The Social Network*'s opening scene shows the opposite budget. The dialogue never stops, so listen for the narrow gaps where sound still gets to work. [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=VUFqhChmHCc)

Discussion: Thom warns against non-stop dialogue, but he praises Tarantino for alternating talky scenes with quiet ones. Is the *Social Network* opening a failure by his standard, or a deliberate trade?

## Ways to invite the ear

Thom lists visual choices that make room for sound: long and short lenses, dutch angles, darkness at the edges of the frame, extreme close-ups and long shots, slow motion, and black and white. Every item on the list withholds picture information. He sums up the common thread himself:

> They all are ways of withholding information.

> Starving the eye will inevitably bring the ear, and therefore the imagination, more into play.

- Clip: *The Conversation* opens on Union Square through a telephoto lens. This is Thom's own example. The flattened depth of field puts us in a subjective space, which lets the track carry sounds that have little to do with what is in the frame. [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wlwdpNw1FW8)
- Clip: *Raging Bull* uses slow motion as dream space, another of Thom's named examples. [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Tx-kB1KKLJ0)
- Clip: *Lawrence of Arabia*'s long shots let us hear the emptiness of a landscape. [Watch](https://youtu.be/HlUFxO0wxVQ?si=JvkspdHORuQg58rd)

Discussion: pick one clip we watched. Which withholding move is it using, and what does the sound fill in?

## Characters need to listen

> Characters need to have the opportunity to listen.

Thom argues that a film gains sound opportunities when its characters notice sound: a pause after the dialogue ends, a scene where someone hears something before seeing it, a place with a voice of its own.

- Clip: *A Quiet Place* opens on a family whose survival depends on hearing each other. Notice how much plot arrives through listening rather than through lines. [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4CmmKmlXD9I)

Discussion: in your screening film, find one moment where a character listens. What does the audience learn there that no line of dialogue delivers?

## Is film a visual medium?

> My opinion is that film is definitely not a "visual medium."

He closes with a list of sound's talents: sound can suggest a mood, set a pace, indicate a place or period, clarify plot, define character, connect ideas, heighten or soften realism, mark time changes, smooth or emphasize transitions, describe an acoustic space, and startle or soothe an audience, usually several of these at once.

Discussion: do you agree with Thom, and did you agree before this week?

## So what does a sound designer do?

Thom's answer is a person, not a job description. Walter Murch and the others at American Zoetrope imagined someone who would work on a film from the script through the final mix, brainstorming with the writer and director in pre-production, protecting sound on the set, then building and coordinating the track in post.

> On "THX-1138" and "The Conversation" Walter Murch was the Sound Designer in the fullest sense of the word.

Murch also cut picture on *The Conversation* and *Apocalypse Now*, which is what let him shape those films around sound. One of his habits: he spends part of his picture editing time with the sync sound turned off, so he cuts toward the track he imagines rather than the rough one he has. That leaves the pauses where sound can do something.

Thom's verdict on the job title in 1999 is blunt, and it is worth asking whether it has changed:

> The term sound designer has come to be associated simply with using specialized equipment to make "special" sound effects.

- Clip: *Apocalypse Now*'s opening. Thom points out that what people praise in this mix "began long before anybody got near a dubbing stage. In fact, it began with the script, and with Coppola's inclination to give the characters in 'Apocalypse' the opportunity to listen to the world around them." [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ntPHFVWDIqM)

His last line widens the job to everyone in the room:

> anybody who shapes sound, edits sound, or even considers sound when making a creative decision in another craft is, at least in a limited sense, designing sound for the movie, and designing the movie for sound.

Discussion: by that definition, who on a student crew is designing sound? Name the decisions in your own projects that were sound decisions without anyone calling them that.

## What's changed since 1999

{{< drill label="Context" >}}

**Some films really were built around sound.** On *Sound of Metal*, director Darius Marder called sound designer Nicolas Becker a year before shooting and the two spent a week working out how to represent a "point of hearing," then sat in an anechoic chamber to hear what silence does to the body ([CNN](https://www.cnn.com/style/article/sound-of-metal-nicolas-becker-mikkel-nielsen-sound-design-spc-intl), [Deadline](https://deadline.com/2021/01/sound-of-metal-sound-designer-nicolas-becker-amazon-studios-interview-news-1234663056/)). On *Baby Driver*, the songs were written into the script years ahead and cleared before filming, so the picture could be choreographed to them rather than cut against them ([Variety](https://variety.com/2018/film/awards/baby-driver-steered-by-choreography-1202707188/), [IndieWire](https://www.indiewire.com/awards/industry/baby-driver-edgar-wright-sound-design-music-1201895451/)). On *Dunkirk*, the script itself "was written to accommodate the auditory illusion of a Shepard tone" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dunkirk_%28soundtrack%29)). 

**The Academy merged its sound categories.** In 2020 the sound branch collapsed Sound Editing and Sound Mixing into one Best Sound award ([Variety](https://variety.com/2020/artisans/news/sound-mixing-oscars-categories-sound-editing-1234592204/)). *Sound of Metal* won the first one in April 2021 ([Deadline](https://deadline.com/2021/04/sound-of-metal-wins-2021-oscars-best-sound-1234743192/)). Sound professionals split on it, some reading it as recognition that the craft is one thing, others as losing half their representation ([Hollywood Reporter](https://www.hollywoodreporter.com/movies/movie-news/sound-pros-evaluate-relaunch-best-sound-oscar-4173811/)).

**Theatrical sound got a new grammar.** Dolby Atmos arrived in June 2012 with *Brave* and moved mixing from fixed channels to objects positioned in a room, with height added overhead ([Forbes](https://www.forbes.com/sites/ericsavitz/2012/04/25/disneypixars-brave-first-film-with-dolby-atmos-audio/), [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dolby_Atmos)).

**Meanwhile the average listener got worse speakers.** A Preply survey found 61% of Americans saying dialogue is harder to hear than it used to be, up from 55% in 2022, with 78% blaming loud background music, and a majority now watching with subtitles on ([Preply](https://preply.com/en/blog/america-still-obsessed-subtitles/), [RTE Brainstorm](https://www.rte.ie/brainstorm/2024/0913/1380770-subtitles-tv-sound-volume-quality-dialogue-technology/), [IndieWire](https://www.indiewire.com/features/general/why-more-people-use-subtitles-1234875864/)).

**Machine learning changed what can be pulled back out of a recording.** Peter Jackson's team built a de-mixing system called MAL for *Get Back*, training a neural network to separate voices and instruments out of old mono tapes ([Variety](https://variety.com/2021/music/news/peter-jackson-get-back-restored-beatles-footage-sound-1235125819)). The same approach lifted John Lennon's vocal off a 1970s demo for "Now and Then" in 2023 ([NPR](https://www.npr.org/sections/world-cafe/2023/11/02/1208848690/the-beatles-last-song-now-and-then)). Thom never had to think about what it means that a finished mix can be taken apart again.

Discussion: Thom's complaint was that sound arrives too late to influence anything. Given the films above, has that been fixed, or did it just become possible for directors who already cared?
