The World Soundscape Project began at Simon Fraser University in the late 1960s
and early 1970s. R. Murray Schafer and a group of composers and students set out
to study how people relate to the sounds around them. Their work included *The
Vancouver Soundscape*, a 1973 recording tour across Canada, the CBC series
*Soundscapes of Canada*, and a study of five European villages.

The project did more than preserve sounds. It asked listeners to notice how a
place changes, which sounds a community values, and which sounds disappear. That
work also led to soundscape composition, in which environmental recordings are
selected, edited, layered, or transformed without losing all connection to their
sources.

## Start with Wednesday's walk

- Which sound did you notice only because you stopped and listened?
- Which sounds acted as a constant background, which demanded attention, and
  which felt specific to that place?
- Did any sound feel worth preserving? Would everyone in the community agree?
- How did your presence, route, and attention shape the soundscape you recorded
  in your journal?

## Key terms

- **Keynote sound:** a persistent background sound that helps define a place,
  such as ventilation, distant traffic, or wind.
- **Signal:** a foreground sound meant to attract attention, such as an alarm,
  bell, or horn.
- **Soundmark:** a sound that a community recognizes as distinctive or valuable.
  It is the sonic counterpart of a landmark.
- **Acoustic ecology:** the study of relationships among listeners, communities,
  and their sonic environments.

Try applying these terms to the sounds from the walk. A sound can change roles
with distance and context. A bell might be background for someone who hears it
every day, a signal when it marks the hour, and a soundmark when the community
identifies with it.

## Listen together

These recordings move from documenting a place toward composing with its
sounds. As you listen, note when you hear selection, sequencing, layering, or
transformation.

1. [Vancouver Harbour Ambience](https://www.sfu.ca/sonic-studio-webdav/excerpts/HarbourAmbience.mp3)
   focuses attention on the acoustic identity of the harbour. What tells you
   about the size, activity, and geography of the place?
2. [The Music of Horns and Whistles](https://www.sfu.ca/sonic-studio-webdav/excerpts/Vanscape5.mp3)
   arranges pitched signals from Vancouver's port and rail environment. When
   does documentation begin to sound like music?
3. [Vancouver Soundmarks](https://www.sfu.ca/sonic-studio-webdav/excerpts/Vanscape6.mp3)
   presents sounds selected for their local identity. Which qualities make a
   sound memorable enough to become a soundmark?
4. [Pacific Fanfare](https://www.sfu.ca/sonic-studio-webdav/excerpts/PacificFanfare.mp3),
   by Barry Truax, works more freely with Vancouver's environmental sounds. Can
   you still recognize the sources? What meaning remains when a sound changes?
5. [Opening of *Island*](https://www.sfu.ca/sonic-studio-webdav/excerpts/Island%20Ex.mp3),
   by Barry Truax, combines recognizable waves with transformed versions of the
   same material. Where does the scene move from real to imagined?

The [*Soundscapes of Canada* program list](https://www.sfu.ca/sonic-studio/WSPDatabase/WSP/canada.html)
describes the ten CBC broadcasts made from the 1973 cross-country recording
tour. The [Soundscape Vancouver archive](https://www.sfu.ca/sonic-studio/WSPDatabase/WSP/vanscape.html)
places the Vancouver recordings and later compositions in context.

## Further listening

If we choose one longer work for Friday, start with *Kits Beach Soundwalk*. It
connects directly to our walk and makes the composer's movement between an
observed place and an imagined inner soundscape easy to hear.

- [*Kits Beach Soundwalk*](https://empreintesdigitales.bandcamp.com/track/kits-beach-soundwalk),
  Hildegard Westerkamp. A narrated walk moves from Vancouver's traffic and
  waves into the amplified, transformed sounds of barnacles.
- [*Lighthouse Park Soundwalk*](https://cec.sonus.ca/Radio/Long/Westerkamp.html),
  Hildegard Westerkamp. An early radio soundwalk that combines the changing
  forest soundscape, Westerkamp's voice, and writing by Emily Carr.
- [*A Walk Through the City*](https://cba.media/407682), Hildegard Westerkamp.
  Recordings from a Vancouver neighborhood, a poem by Norbert Ruebsaat, and
  studio processing turn a walk into a shifting urban narrative.
- [*Beneath the Forest Floor*](https://thelisteningroom1.bandcamp.com/album/beneath-the-forest-floor),
  Hildegard Westerkamp. West Coast forest recordings move between documentary
  detail and an imagined inner forest. Excerpts later appeared in Gus Van
  Sant's *Elephant*.
- [*Talking Rain*](https://earsaymusic.bandcamp.com/track/talking-rain),
  Hildegard Westerkamp. Listen for changes in the density and texture of rain,
  and for the transitions that make each new place audible.

## Discussion

1. Can a field recording ever be neutral? Consider microphone placement, the
   route taken, what the recordist chose to capture, and what was edited out.
2. What is gained when an environmental sound remains recognizable? What becomes
   possible when it is transformed?
3. Schafer's project joined listening with concern about noise and environmental
   change. Who gets to decide whether a sound is unwanted noise or a valued
   soundmark?
4. Which parts of this work resemble what a film sound designer does when
   building a place for a scene?
5. Choose one moment from Wednesday's walk. Which sounds would you preserve,
   remove, exaggerate, or transform if the scene were realistic? What would you
   change if the scene followed a character's subjective experience?
